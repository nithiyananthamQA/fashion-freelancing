03 — Which loom

Dobby or jacquard — we tell you straight.

It is not a style choice. It is who controls the warp: a shaft that moves a whole group of ends, or a hook that moves one.

Group controlDobby

Ends are threaded onto shafts, and a shaft lifts every end on it together. Eight to thirty-two shafts is typical — that count is the ceiling.

  • Twills, satins, waffle, piqué, bedford cord, small geometrics
  • Stripes and checks built from a handful of end groups
  • Cheapest to set up, and most mills in the cluster have one

End-by-end controlJacquard

Every end hangs on its own hook, so no two ends need agree. The limit is hook count and repeat width, not shafts.

  • Damask, large florals, figures, borders, logos
  • Structures used as a palette — satin catches light where twill holds shadow
  • Costlier to set, so the design should earn the loom

The test we apply: if two ends side by side must behave differently, no number of shafts will do it — that is jacquard. If a few repeating end groups can build it, it belongs on a dobby, and we will say so.

04 — Colourway economics

Change the weft, not the warp.

Swapping a weft cone takes minutes. Re-beaming a warp takes a day and a bill, so the range should come out of the weft wherever it can.

Swapping weft yarns between colourways is quick and cheap. Re-beaming a warp is neither. So the range is engineered to come out of weft changes — and when a colourway truly needs its own warp, we flag it before you commit.

Warp-way stripes are the exception. A stripe running down the cloth lives in the warp, so changing it means a new beam. We tell you which colourways share one.
